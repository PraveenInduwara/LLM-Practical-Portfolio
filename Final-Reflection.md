# Final Reflection

Across the PUSL3205 practicals, I moved from conventional text classification to building and evaluating systems that generate text, retrieve information, and use tools. The main lesson for me is that a successful run or a good-looking metric is only one part of evaluating a language system. I also need to check whether its output is useful, supported by evidence, and safe for the task.

In Lab 1, I used bag-of-words and TF-IDF features with logistic regression for sentiment classification. Both approaches reached 0.822 accuracy on the recorded split, which gave me a clear baseline and helped me understand how preprocessing and feature choices affect a small NLP task. The result applies to that dataset and split; it does not establish that the same classifier can answer open-ended questions.

Lab 2 changed my focus from a model experiment to a product idea. ShareShelf became a mobile-first prototype for pantry tracking, food sharing, and chats with friends. Breaking the idea into users and features helped me connect language technology to a practical interface. The prototype remains a local demonstration: it does not provide real multi-user coordination or evidence of reduced food waste. I learned to distinguish a persuasive concept and interface from a tested service.

In Lab 3, I implemented a character-level Transformer and saw training loss fall, but the generated stories still contained malformed words and grammar. That result made the difference between next-token prediction and readable generation concrete. Lab 4 extended the work to QLoRA fine-tuning. The adapter trained and produced measurable loss and perplexity, but comparisons on held-out and unseen questions showed mixed, sometimes unsafe answers. I learned that fine-tuning metrics and a saved adapter do not replace careful answer-level evaluation.

Lab 5 was the clearest practical example of grounding answers in documents. Comparing fixed-size, section-aware, and parent-child chunking showed that the retrieval method affected answer quality, even when retrieval scores were similar. Manual review, citation checks, and latency measurements revealed limitations that a single automatic score would miss. The tests were small and used a public handbook excerpt rather than NSBM material, so I cannot generalize their results to university support. The live Streamlit upload/chat demonstration and screenshots are still outstanding.

Lab 6 combined several earlier ideas in a tool-using agent. I connected a local model to calculator, date/time, file, memory, RAG, and Calendar tools. The allowlist and approval checks showed why permissions should be enforced in application code. The passing automated checks and verified Calendar action are useful engineering evidence, but they do not establish reliability with real university policies or a wider student population. Visual screenshots for this lab also remain to be captured.

These practicals strengthened my skills in Python, notebooks, model training, retrieval, evaluation, documentation, and version control. They also changed how I assess system quality: I should define the task first, compare against a simpler baseline, inspect representative outputs, record limitations, and avoid claims that exceed the evidence. If I continued the work, I would complete and capture the Lab 5 live demonstration, add Lab 6 visual evidence, and test any student-facing system with current approved documents and representative users before proposing deployment.
